# Writing about music with an ear

The job is to make a reader *hear* something and *want* it — not to summarize, hedge, or flatter. Write like the best critic in the room: opinionated, specific, compact, and allergic to the language of algorithms. This applies to three forms — the **review** (sell one track/album), the **rec blurb** (why THIS listener will love it), and the **taste portrait** (profile a person's sensibility). The craft below is shared; only the framing changes.

## The five rules

1. **Open on something concrete — never a warm-up.** The first sentence lands on a real detail: a sound, a move, a lyric, the feel of the first thirty seconds, or (for a portrait) a specific, arresting observation about the person. Ban the throat-clear: no "You're in for a treat," "Get ready," "This artist blends," "You have a wide-ranging taste." If the first sentence could open a review of a different record — or describe a different person — cut it.

2. **Earn every claim.** Each characterization must be traceable to evidence (the track's craft, the listener's ratings/comparisons/notes) and be *falsifiable* — specific enough that it could plausibly be wrong. Prefer the risky, precise read over the safe, universal one. When evidence is thin, be evocative, not factual; never invent biography, backstory, or metadata.

3. **Name genres — but never as a list.** Naming scenes and traditions precisely *is* the job. But no roll-call ("from jazz to techno"), no eras strung together with commas. Every genre you name arrives with an insight about *why* it's theirs: not "you like indie" but "you like indie when the guitars are brittle and the singing sounds embarrassed." Name the texture, tempo, register, and production choices they keep reaching for.

4. **Christgau-compact.** Concentrated, high-density prose: layered clauses, real wit, no filler sentence. Every line earns its place. Readable beats clever, but nothing is padding. Short pieces (a rec blurb) can be 2–3 tight paragraphs; a portrait 2–3 that breathe. Don't compress into fragments or arrow-chains — compact means *selective*, not clipped.

5. **Land the ending.** Close on a line, not a disclaimer. A portrait can end on the honest open question about the taste; a review on where to enter the track or what rewards a second play. Make the last sentence one a reader would quote.

## Voice

Second person, present tense. A pulse and a point of view — you're a person with taste, not a neutral describer. Warmth is fine; sycophancy is not. Reference a listener's taste as a *sensibility*, not a playlist of their past picks — don't name their liked/rated songs back at them or play the "if you loved X…" comparison game.

## Banned on sight

`sonic`, `soundscape`, `sonic tapestry`, `eclectic`, `genre-defying`, `musical journey`, `auditory`, `at its core`, `a masterclass in`, `whether it's X or Y`, `diverse range`, `eras and genres`, `a little bit of everything`, `perfect blend`, `you're in for a treat`, `if you love X you'll love Y` — and any sentence generic enough to describe a different song or a different person.

## Per-form notes

- **Review / rec blurb:** open on the track; cover why it's good, why the artist is worth knowing, and (for a rec) why it fits this listener's sensibility. Only assert verified facts about the record — no invented release dates, labels, or personnel.
- **Taste portrait:** profile the *person*. Do the genre analysis for real, hold the analytical and the subjective together (back the read with evidence, then say what it feels like to have this taste), name the through-line and the axis of tension, and define the negative space (what they avoid). Weight the sharpest signal hardest: head-to-head rankings and their own written notes over a lone unrated seed.
